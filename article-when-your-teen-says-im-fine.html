When Your Teen Says “I’m Fine”

Learning to hear what they may not know how to say

Boladale Gold, LCPCMoving Beyond My Brokenness

There’s a particular kind of confusion that comes over a parent when you look at a child you know so well and realize you have no idea what’s actually going on inside them anymore.

You ask if they’re okay. “I’m fine.” You ask about school. “Nothing happened.” You ask why they’ve been holed up in their room for weeks. “I just want to be alone.”

And most of the time, we let those answers stand — because teenagers are supposed to want more privacy, more distance, more room to become their own person. That’s normal. That’s growing up.

But every once in a while, “I’m fine” doesn’t mean nothing is wrong. It means I don’t know how to explain this, or I’m afraid you won’t understand, or I don’t want you to worry, or honestly, I don’t fully understand it myself yet.

Here’s something I’ve come to believe deeply, both as a clinician and as a mother: our children don’t always tell us what’s happening in words. A lot of the time, their behavior says it first.

Behavior Is Often the First Language

Adolescence is messy by design. Kids pull away, friendships take center stage, moods swing hard, and disagreements with parents become part of the furniture. None of that automatically means something is wrong. We shouldn’t go looking for a diagnosis behind every slammed door.

But we also can’t wave away every real shift with “teenagers will be teenagers.” Withdrawal from friends and family, sudden changes in sleep or appetite, losing interest in things they used to love, a drop in grades, a growing sense of hopelessness, substance use, recklessness, or a personality that just feels off from the child you know — these are worth paying attention to.

Usually the real question isn’t “is this normal teenage behavior,” but something closer to: has my child become noticeably different from who I know them to be — and if so, what changed?

Get Curious Before You Correct

Our instinct as parents is to correct. Grades slip, and we say “you need to focus.” They stay locked in their room, and we say “you’re spending too much time in there.” They snap at us, and we say “watch your tone.”

None of that is wrong on its own — boundaries matter, and mental health awareness isn’t a license for kids to do whatever they want. But there’s a second question worth holding alongside the correction: what’s happening underneath this?

A teenager who stops turning in homework isn’t always just being lazy. A kid with a stomachache every school morning isn’t always avoiding responsibility. A once-social teen who suddenly disappears into isolation isn’t always just asserting independence. And an angry teenager is sometimes communicating fear, or grief, or bullying, or anxiety — because anger is a far easier thing to show than pain is.

Correct what needs correcting. But stay curious about what needs understanding.

Why Some Teenagers Go Quiet

One of the harder questions we can ask ourselves isn’t “why won’t my child talk to me?” It’s “what does my child think will happen if they tell me the truth?”

Will I lecture right away? Panic? Tell my sister, my mother, half the church? Minimize it? Compare them to their cousin who “never had these problems”? Turn it into a speech about everything I’ve sacrificed for them? Punish first and ask questions later?

Or will I actually listen?

How we react the first time matters enormously, because it shapes whether there’s a second time. That doesn’t mean parents should sit on genuinely dangerous secrets — safety always comes first. But there’s a real difference between responding to what your child needs in that moment and reacting out of your own fear.

Your Reaction Today Shapes What They Tell You Tomorrow

Picture your teenager finally working up the nerve to say, “Mom, I think I’m depressed.” A frightened parent might blurt out, “Depressed? You have everything!” What we usually mean by that is I love you, I’ve worked so hard to give you a good life, I don’t understand how you could feel this way. But what the teenager often hears is: my feelings don’t make sense, so I should stop bringing them up.

Sometimes the better response is much smaller. “Thank you for telling me.” “I want to understand this.” “How long have you felt this way?” “You don’t have to carry this by yourself.”

Then you listen. You don’t have to fix everything in that first conversation. Sometimes just keeping the door open is the whole win.

Don’t Turn Every Conversation Into an Interrogation

It’s easy, out of sheer worry, to start firing off questions — what happened, who did this, when did it start, why didn’t you tell me sooner, what else are you not telling me — until your teenager starts to feel like they’re being cross-examined instead of cared for.

There’s a place for those questions eventually. But connection often starts somewhere softer: “I’ve noticed you haven’t seemed like yourself. You don’t have to talk about it right now, but I want you to know I’m here whenever you’re ready.” And then you back that up with your actions — sitting together, driving somewhere with no agenda, cooking, taking a walk, watching a show. Ordinary moments where conversation can happen naturally, instead of your child feeling like they’ve been summoned to a meeting.

Trust is almost always built in those ordinary moments, long before you ever need it in the extraordinary ones.

When “I’m Fine” Calls for More Than a Conversation

There’s also a point where a parent shouldn’t just wait patiently for their teenager to feel ready to talk. Significant changes in functioning, persistent depression or anxiety, substance misuse, self-harm, serious behavioral shifts, hopelessness, talk of death or suicide, giving away belongings, or statements about others being “better off” without them — these call for professional evaluation, not a wait-and-see approach. Any mention of suicide should be taken seriously, never brushed off as drama or attention-seeking.

Getting your child professional help isn’t a mark against you as a parent. Sometimes it’s one of the most responsible things you can do for them.

Faith and Therapy Aren’t Competing Teams

For families of faith, I want to say plainly: we can pray and seek professional help. We can speak Scripture over our children and take them to therapy. We can believe God heals and still recognize that treatment is often part of how that healing shows up.

Our children shouldn’t have to choose between looking spiritually strong and admitting they’re struggling. There’s room for both faith and honesty in the same household.

Build the Home They’ll Want to Come Back To

We can’t control everything our children run into. We can’t prevent every disappointment or heartbreak, and as they get older, we won’t have access to every thought in their heads. But we do have real influence over one thing: what it feels like for them to come home and tell us the truth.

Let your home be a place where mistakes can be talked about without humiliation, where hard emotions aren’t automatically branded disrespect, where mental illness isn’t the punchline of a joke, and where asking for counseling is never treated as weakness. A place where consequences might still come, but love was never in question. A place where a teenager can finally say, “something isn’t right,” and know that sentence won’t blow up the relationship.

A Final Word

Don’t assume silence means peace. Don’t assume good grades mean everything’s fine. Don’t assume the kid who’s laughing with friends can’t be hurting underneath it. And don’t wait for a crisis before you start having these conversations.

Ask. Listen. Watch closely. Stay close. And when it’s time, get help.

Because sometimes the child who says “I’m fine” is really just waiting to find out whether someone loves them enough to hear what they haven’t figured out how to say yet.

Instead of only asking “how do I change my teenager’s behavior,” maybe the better question is: what is my child’s behavior trying to tell me — and have I made enough room for them to say the rest?
